    How the pipeline works

    Aim 1 produces the measurements (Orbach lab); Aim 2 turns them into ranked network modules (Guo lab).

    Step 1

    Liver spheroids

    HepaRG cells self-assemble into 200 µm spheroids that keep drug-metabolizing enzymes active.

    Step 2

    Cyclophosphamide exposure

    Control, ½ LC50 and LC50 for 24 h; three pooled replicates per condition.

    Step 3

    Three readouts

    LDH for cell death, bulk RNA-seq for gene activity, a 105-cytokine array for inflammatory signals.

    Step 4

    Map onto STRING

    Measurements become node weights on a human protein interaction network.

    Step 5

    Search for modules

    A scalable subgraph search finds compact, connected, dose-responsive modules and ranks them.

    Why this matters

    Drug-induced liver injury is a leading cause of dose reduction and treatment interruption in cancer care, and patients on the same drug respond very differently. Single-gene biomarkers capture only a fragment of the biology. Network modules are more robust, point directly at mechanisms, and generate testable hypotheses for follow-up studies and larger NIH and NSF applications.

    Methods behind the prototype (what the real pipeline will do)

    Raw counts are filtered and normalized; treatment effects are estimated per gene with a count-based model (DESeq2). The STRING human network is loaded at a recorded version and confidence threshold. Cytokines from the array are linked to their encoding genes but kept as a separate evidence layer. Module discovery is an optimization over connected subgraphs balancing response strength, interaction support, compactness and cross-layer agreement; a greedy grow-and-refine search seeded at high-scoring nodes is compared with top-gene ranking and community-detection baselines. Modules are tested against size- and degree-matched random gene sets, and their stability is checked by varying thresholds and leaving out samples. Module activity per sample is the mean normalized expression of its members.
